# One Front Door, Many Rooms: Brand, Marketing and Sales Structure for OurCommunityRituals

**Bottom line:** Don't run two consumer front doors (one anonymous, one named). Run **one master brand that hosts, sponsors and attendees all see**, with plain-language "doors" named for each audience: Host, Sponsor, Check-in, Stage, Report. Keep the "ourShared…" family as internal engineering names only. Give a separately endorsed brand to just two audiences whose needs really are different: enterprise/academic data buyers and the consulting practice. Go to market host-first, one node at a time. Run sales as a channel-led, kit-based motion through institutions that already group the people you need: parish groupings, library systems, chambers and league operators. Your first hire should be a field "node launcher," not a salesperson.

## TL;DR

- **Brand:** Use a branded house. The public master brand is "Shared Choice" (OurSharedChoice, which the earlier screen found low-to-moderate risk), with descriptive doors: _Shared Choice for Hosts / for Sponsors / Check-in / Stage / Report_. Retire OurUniqueThought, and keep OurCommunityRituals as the legal and editorial umbrella only, not a consumer trademark. A new screen puts it at moderate-to-high risk because Rituals Cosmetics actively opposes "ritual" marks and there is a pending RITUALS application for live events. Avoid "Show of Hands" and "Headcount": both are high-conflict.
- **Marketing:** Lead with the choice ("Keep Facebook. Add a choice.") rather than the privacy technology. Your older volunteer audience is still on Facebook: per Pew (2025), 71% of U.S. adults and 57% of those 65+ use it. Market _on_ Facebook to win the choice _off_ it. Segment businesses by who they are and attendees only by aggregate pattern (event type × H3 cell × time). The north-star leading indicator is each node's direct-scan/visit to Facebook-referral ratio.
- **Sales:** Use a founder-led consulting wedge until it's repeatable, then a channel-plus-kit motion, with manual sponsor invoicing as the "sales" core. Hire in this order: (1) node launcher / community manager, (2) institutional partnerships lead, (3) sponsor success and billing operations, (4) report and data analyst, (5) marketing and content lead. Hold the investor conversation until you can show two or three nodes that tipped without you in the room.

## Key Findings

1. **The research favors a single umbrella brand when the products share their underlying technology and the audiences are related but not identical. That describes OCR's host/sponsor/attendee core.** Jungju Yu's model (_Marketing Science_, 2021) finds umbrella branding optimal "if the supply-side relatedness is high and demand-relatedness is not too high." Hosts, sponsors and attendees share one platform and one event but want different things, which is the umbrella sweet spot. Data buyers differ on both dimensions: a different product (warehouse queries) and a different buyer (BI and academic). That supports giving them an endorsed sub-brand. Practitioner guidance points the same way: DeepMarketing.it's 2026 brand-architecture guide says that for a small or medium-sized business a branded house "concentrates investment on a single strong brand and avoids budget fragmentation." Some advisers also note that in high-risk categories like privacy, separate brands isolate reputational damage. That second point is the argument for keeping the _data_ product at arm's length from the community brand.

2. **The anonymous/named split belongs in the architecture and the law, not in consumer branding.** Your legal privacy wall (separate repos) is real and should stay. But an attendee at a parish fish fry should not have to learn two brand names to understand one event. Block offers a useful example in both directions. In December 2021 Square, Inc. renamed itself Block so that "the full Square brand" could go to the seller business while Cash App served consumers. Separate audience brands worked there, but only with a huge budget and a parent company behind them. A solo founder cannot fund two consumer brands' awareness. Atlassian went the other way and moved its 14+ products toward attribution lockups that "feel unified under one parent brand." Copy the Atlassian pattern: one parent, plainly descriptive product doors.

3. **Network businesses that won locally all started smaller than they expected and courted the "hard side" first.** Andrew Chen's _Cold Start Problem_ defines the atomic network as "the smallest, stable network from which all other networks can be built" and says "acquiring the hard side of the network and keeping them happy is paramount." For OCR, the hard side is hosts. Examples:
   - **Nextdoor** required founding members to recruit a minimum of 10 households before a neighborhood went live. It reached 6,672 neighborhoods by the end of 2012, and when it launched in New York City in June 2013, more than 1,800 neighborhood sites were created almost immediately.
   - **Yelp** focused only on San Francisco for its first year. Its first community manager, Nish Nadaraja, was the fifth hire and "the first non-technical person," and Yelp founded its Elite program in 2005, with Elite events about monthly.
   - **Partiful** hires student "Campus Growth Managers" to "identify and convert event hosts (students, student orgs, venues)" during a launch week.
   - **Luma** grew through host-owned "calendars with subscribers." Co-founder Victor Pontis told Business Insider in May 2024 that "around 2 million people sign up for events on the app each month" and that the user base grew fivefold in a year.

4. **How you monetize the organizer side is the biggest long-run trust risk in this category.** In 2005, when Meetup started charging organizers, activity dropped 95% before recovering. After Bending Spoons acquired Meetup in 2024, it raised organizer fees from $24 to $45 a month and paywalled features, according to Morning Brew (March 2026). OCR's model avoids this today: participation is free and only the report is paid. The marketing has to make that a public, lasting promise, and the change-of-control clause should cover pricing posture as well as data.

5. **Self-serve beats a heavy sales force at the long tail, but institutions still need people.** Before COVID, Eventbrite courted large creators with "lots of hand holding" and spent 31.5% of sales on sales and marketing in 2019. After it pivoted to self-service, that fell to 19% in 2022. Lesson: build the kit so a volunteer can self-serve, but put human relationships where buying is collective (dioceses, municipalities, league operators, sponsors).

6. **Privacy-first brands grow slowly, then compound. They win on a relatable benefit and transparency, not on specifications.** Plausible Analytics took 324 days to reach $400 MRR, crossed $1M ARR in June 2022, and was estimated at about $3.1M ARR by late 2024, all bootstrapped. Its most durable channel was public transparency about revenue and decisions. DuckDuckGo's searches roughly doubled (54.4M to 105.6M per month) right after the 2013 Snowden revelations, so news events are when privacy messaging lands. Apple's 2019 "Privacy. That's iPhone." campaign ran billboards with lines like "What happens on your iPhone, stays on your iPhone." Its first TV spot, per Fast Company, skipped features and competitor-bashing and instead showed everyday moments of wanting privacy. One pitfall: Facebook COO Sheryl Sandberg argued on an investor call that Apple's iOS tracking changes favored Apple's own ad business, and Proton wrote that "ATT may have been more about blocking competitors than protecting user privacy." Apple answered with an April 2022 white paper by Columbia's Kinshuk Jerath arguing its Search Ads growth "predated the introduction of ATT." Any perceived gap between promise and incentive gets punished, so your legible-rigor page matters more than any slogan.

7. **Your first institutional channel is already organized for you.** The Archdiocese of Chicago's Facts and Figures page lists 216 parishes as of January 2026, down from "about 350 parishes in the archdiocese in 2016" per CNA/National Catholic Register. They are organized into roughly 100 Renew My Church "groupings" ("activated since 2016," per Our Lady of Hope Parish's Renew My Church page) of two to eight parishes, across six vicariates and 35 deaneries. A grouping is a ready-made atomic network: geographically close parishes that already share staff, calendars and parishioners. Churches are Facebook-native: in 2017, 84% of Protestant pastors (LifeWay) said their church uses Facebook. The choice-not-demand pitch therefore fits how these institutions already behave.

**Should the internal "ourShared…" names be public?** No. OSE, OSM, OSV, ourAirwaves, ourSharedMosaics and the rest are excellent engineering vocabulary, but they are layer names, not doors. A parish volunteer needs "Check-in" and "Report," not "Magnets" and "Mosaics." Expose them only as feature names inside the product where they describe something the user does. For example, "Themes" can replace Magnets/Airwaves in the UI, and "Mosaic view" can survive as a display mode.

**Naming principles for local-community products:**

- **Speakable over the phone.** Someone should be able to say it once at a parish council meeting and have it spelled correctly.
- **Descriptive doors, distinctive master.** Trademark law ranks names from generic and descriptive (weak, often unregistrable without "secondary meaning") through suggestive, arbitrary and fanciful (inherently distinctive). And "crowded fields weaken all marks." So the _master_ name must carry the distinctiveness, while doors can be plain descriptors that never need to be registered.
- **About the "Our…" prefix:** it is warm and communal but weak and crowded in civic tech, so don't make it carry legal weight. Say "Shared Choice" out loud and keep "ourSharedChoice" as the domain and handle where it's available.

### 3. Go-to-market structure for a cold-start, multi-sided local platform

**Sequence the sides: hosts, then attendees, then sponsors, then report buyers.** Hosts are the hard side. Attendees come with the events, and the QR or short link is the acquisition loop, in the way that every Partiful invite exposes guests to the brand. Sponsors come once there are recurring events with countable, aggregate turnout. Report revenue follows sponsors.

**Define the atomic network concretely:** one _node_ = one recurring host venue plus at least 3 recurring event series plus at least 1 confirmed sponsor, inside one H3 neighborhood cluster. Rock Island Public House (record nights, games) is a node seed. A Renew My Church parish grouping (fest, fish fry, bingo) is a node seed. Blue Island's city calendar is a civic node seed.

**Choice-then-saturation is the right doctrine. Instrument it.** Every host's Facebook post carries a channel-tagged short link. That gives you, per node and fully in aggregate, the share of check-ins and visits arriving via Facebook versus direct scan, bookmark or calendar subscription. A node is "saturated" when direct exceeds Facebook referrals for 8 consecutive weeks. That is your tipping-point signal, and it is a privacy-safe one.

**What the comparables teach:**

- Like Nextdoor, set a **minimum-viable threshold** before calling a node live (e.g., 3 series plus 1 sponsor). It also protects the k-anonymity floor, because tiny events produce no report.
- Like Yelp, put a **human community manager in each metro** and make them the local face. Recognition can work like the Elite program but stay anonymous-compatible, e.g., "Founding Host" badges for named organizers on the Stage door.
- Like Luma, make **host-owned calendars subscribable**. That is how attendees return without OCR knowing who they are.
- Like Meetup in 2005, **never charge the participation side**. Write it down publicly.

**Product-led versus sales-assisted:** Use product-led for the long tail (any host can create events and print a green QR). Use sales-assisted for anything collective (parishes as groupings, cities, leagues). Use channel for scale (dioceses, chambers, league companies). OCR is B2B2C: institutions carry the attendee audience to you, so every sales motion is really a distribution deal.

**The "kit" is the product for non-technical buyers.** It's a printed one-pager, pre-made QR placards, a 5-minute volunteer script ("Scan if you'd like to be counted. We never know who."), a Facebook post template with the short link, and a sponsor ask letter. The test is your own doctrine: a volunteer kicks off a node with no founder present.

**First 3–5 non-engineering hires, in order (manual-before-technical):**

1. **Node launcher / community manager** (part-time contractor first, Chicago south side). Recruits hosts, runs kit onboarding, builds Founding Host relationships. This is the Yelp CM and Partiful campus-manager role.
2. **Institutional partnerships lead.** Parish groupings, library systems, Blue Island-type municipalities, chambers. Long-cycle relationship selling.
3. **Sponsor success and billing operations.** Owns manual invoices and renewals and tracks the "12 consecutive manual renewals" gate. This is your real revenue team.
4. **Report and data analyst.** Turns aggregate ledgers into SharedReports that sponsors renew on, and prepares studied-first datasets for academic partners.
5. **Marketing and content lead.** Runs field-proof storytelling, Facebook/Instagram, Substack and the legible-rigor page and changelog.

### 5. Segmenting audiences without "who"

**Business audience:** These are organizations, and they voluntarily identify themselves to sign up, so conventional B2B segmentation applies: type (parish, library, bar, league, civic, sponsor), size, event cadence, geography and wave.

**Attendee audience: segment patterns, never people.** Use only aggregate, k-floored cuts: event type × H3 cell × day-part × season × container shape (parent/child). Attribution comes from the object rather than the person: placement-cell QR codes for print and vicinity-level short links per channel (Facebook post, parish bulletin, flyer, Stage profile). For deeper research, use methods that are opt-in and kept separate from the data plane:

- quarterly anonymous intercept surveys at events (paper or a separate short link, never joined to check-ins);
- recorded interviews with willing hosts and named Stage creators;
- a small volunteer "Neighbors Panel" recruited publicly, explicitly outside OSE.

Publish your research methods on the "How we keep this promise" page.

**Messaging that should land with older, volunteer-run, non-technical organizations:**

- "Keep Facebook. Add a choice." (non-threatening; it matches the fact that 57% of 65+ adults are on Facebook while only 19% use Instagram, per Pew 2025)
- "Never who. Always how many." (keep; it's your best line)
- "Green means counted, never known."
- "Your sponsors see the turnout, not the people."
- "Free to host. Free to sponsor. Pay only for the report."

Avoid "k-anonymity," "H3" and "aggregation" in front-door copy. Put them on the legible-rigor page.

### 6. Investor and funding interplay

Your objective order (cheap adoption, then data, then longevity) is closer to Plausible's patient compounding than to a venture blitz. A national-scale seed investor will want proof that atomic networks repeat. Chen describes investing in Clubhouse "just based on the idea that this one atomic network was working." So the story should be: "Here are three nodes, in three different community types, that tipped (direct exceeded Facebook referrals) and renewed sponsors manually, and the third one launched without me." Friends-and-family money should fund exactly two things: the part-time node launcher and attorney clearance and trademark filing. Raise institutional money only after the node-repeatability evidence exists, so the GTM story drives the raise rather than the reverse. Watch for tension between a growth-maximizing investor and your "studied first, then sold," keep-forever stewardship doctrine. Put the data doctrine in governing documents before any priced round.

## Recommendations

### (1) Brand architecture and naming

- **Master brand (public):** **Shared Choice** (domain and handle ourSharedChoice). Tagline: _"Never who. Always how many."_ Secondary line: _"Events, sponsors, attendees — by choice."_ Brand figure: the choice triangle.
- **Doors (plain descriptors, unregistered):**
  - **Shared Choice for Hosts:** "Post your event, print your green code, keep your Facebook."
  - **Shared Choice for Sponsors:** "Back local events. See turnout, never people. Prepaid, no auto-renew."
  - **Shared Choice Check-in** (green QR): "Scan to be counted. We never know who." Blue QR: "Give — paid directly to the organizer."
  - **Shared Choice Stage** (named layer, replaces OurUniqueThought/OW/OSV): "Plant your flag." For DJs, artists, spoken word, athletes and organizers who choose to be known.
  - **Shared Choice Report** (SharedReport): "The anonymous ledger of your event." Basic, then Mid, then Enterprise.
  - **Map:** "What's on near you," the attendee door.
- **Endorsed sub-brands:** **EventRings, from Shared Choice** (BI and academic warehouse queries). **Pulse, by Community Rituals** (league consulting runbooks). Keep **OSR/OSG** as named field-proof community series ("Record Night," "Games Night") rather than brands.
- **Umbrella:** Keep **OurCommunityRituals LLC** as the legal entity, investor-facing parent and editorial voice. Do not invest consumer brand equity in it or file it as a consumer mark until counsel weighs the RITUALS risk.
- **Drop:** OurUniqueThought; public use of the ourShared… family; Show of Hands, Headcount and Flagpole as candidates; the "Hub" label (it's generic).

### (2) Marketing structure

- **Positioning:** "The choice alongside Facebook for local events that want sponsors without surveillance."
- **Two audiences, two engines.** Business: field proof (case stories from Rock Island Public House, the Eagles lodge games night, the first parish fest), kit downloads and partner talks. Attendee: on-site signage, host posts with short links, subscribable calendars and the Map. No attendee ads beyond what hosts post.
- **Channels:** Facebook/Instagram pages (from October 2026) for host recruitment and proof; Substack for the punk, human founder voice and the public changelog; parish bulletins, library newsletters and chamber emails as earned channels; local press when a news event raises privacy concerns, as DuckDuckGo saw in 2013.
- **Content cadence for one person:** one field-proof story a week (repurposed across FB, IG and Substack), one monthly "promise update" (changelog plus incidents), one quarterly node scoreboard (aggregate only).

### (3) Sales structure

- **Now to the first hire:** founder-led consulting wedge (Pulse) to stand up nodes, then hand over to kits.
- **Motions:** product-led for hosts; sales-assisted for parish groupings, municipalities and leagues; channel/partner for dioceses, chambers and ABC.
- **Revenue engine:** manual sponsor invoices; the report as the paid object; the 12-renewal automation gate.
- **Hires:** node launcher, then partnerships, then sponsor success/billing, then analyst, then marketing.

## Caveats

- Some growth figures come from secondary sources: Plausible's 2023–2024 ARR is a third-party estimate, and Partiful's metrics come from marketing blogs. Treat them as directional.
- The LifeWay figure is for Protestant churches (2017); comparable data for Catholic parishes wasn't found. Archdiocesan parish counts are as of January 2026.

- Roadmap targets are proposed hypotheses for calibration, not industry benchmarks.
